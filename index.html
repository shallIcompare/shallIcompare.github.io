I work on the ethics of artificial intelligence, drawing on philosophy, cognitive science and law. I study how people assign responsibility and blame, and how they decide whether to trust another person or, increasingly, an AI system. My research combines philosophical analysis with large-scale experiments to examine what these judgements mean for how AI should be built, regulated and used.

Research

I develop philosophical accounts of responsibility, communication and moral judgement, and test them through experiments. These studies involve thousands of participants across the US, Europe and Asia, including professional judges. Since 2019, I have focused on how these concepts apply to artificial agents.

Responsibility in human–AI interaction

When an AI system contributes to a harmful decision, who is blamed, and who should be? My colleagues and I have examined how people in the US, Germany and Japan assign responsibility to human operators and AI agents, including whether they attribute intentions and guilt to machines. Related work concerns ‘meaningful human control’: what it requires when people supervise systems that are faster, and sometimes better, than they are. A current paper, In the Loop, Out of Sync, examines when a person involved in an AI system’s decisions has meaningful control over them.

Science and Engineering Ethics 2024 · PACM HCI (CSCW) 2021 · Computers in Human Behavior Reports 2023

Trust and reliance

Trust in automated systems raises two related questions: how people decide how much to trust a system, and when that trust is justified. In experiments involving people working with AI, participants delegated even decisions with moral consequences to capable machines. A survey of 1,300 Swiss residents across five domains found that trust and willingness to delegate varied sharply with the domain and the stakes. Findings from one application do not simply carry over to another.

ACM CHI 2022 · Trust and responsibility in human–AI interaction (in preparation)

Honest communication in the age of AI

When is it acceptable to present something as fact, and when does a false statement count as a lie? This research examines those questions and whether the answers change when the speaker is an AI system. It began with experiments on the standards people expect speakers to meet and whether those standards remain consistent across cultures. The four-country NIHAI consortium continues this work by studying the rules that should govern conversations with AI systems. Related work examines how social media, including the option to repost other people’s content, changes the incentives to tell the truth.

PNAS 2021 · Cognitive Science 2021 · Cognition 2018 · CogSci 2026

Moral judgement, law and alignment

I study how people judge wrongdoing and how the law should respond. Studies with professional judges and lay participants examine the role of outcomes and luck in moral judgement, how people understand reasonableness, and how norms influence judgements about what caused an event.

Recent work extends these questions to AI. People have different moral expectations of human and artificial agents, which makes it unclear what ‘alignment with human values’ should mean. Large language models may also reproduce known biases in legal judgement. This raises a further question about whether those biases can be reduced.

Cognition 2017, 2019, 2023, 2025 · ACM FAccT 2025 · Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics 2026

Lab and projects

Guilty Minds Lab

I founded the Guilty Minds Lab at the University of Zurich in 2019. Now based at the University of Graz, the lab brings together philosophers, cognitive scientists and computer scientists who study moral and legal cognition and the ethics of AI. It has secured several million in competitive funding to date, including from the Swiss National Science Foundation, HERA/CHANSE and Innosuisse.

NIHAI: Rules for conversations with AI

NIHAI brings together researchers in Graz, Zurich, Bucharest and Kraków to study conversations between people and AI systems. We examine what people expect from these conversations, how they judge systems that violate those expectations, and what principles should guide the design of conversational agents. The project is funded by HERA/CHANSE for 2025–2028.

Talks, policy and advisory

I give talks and advise academic audiences, policymakers, international organisations and companies on AI and its implications for responsibility, trust and decision-making.

I have given more than 130 talks at universities and research institutes and at practitioner and policy forums. These have included talks at Oxford, Cambridge, Yale, Princeton, Columbia, NYU, the École Normale Supérieure, ETH Zurich and several Max Planck Institutes, as well as contributions to United Nations discussions on autonomous weapons.

Alongside keynotes and lectures, I lead workshops and advise international organisations, government agencies and private companies on AI governance, responsibility and human oversight.

Topics

  • Responsibility. Who is to blame when an AI system contributes to harm, how ‘responsibility gaps’ arise, and how they can be closed.
  • Human oversight. What meaningful human control requires when an AI system works faster, and sometimes performs better, than the person supervising it.
  • Trust and reliance. When people hand decisions over to AI, and why trust varies sharply with the domain and the stakes.
  • Alignment. How differences between our expectations of people and AI systems complicate the aim of aligning AI with human values.
  • AI in high-stakes judgement. Whether AI systems reproduce familiar human biases in legal and similarly consequential decisions, and whether those biases can be reduced.
  • Honest AI. What conversational AI systems should be allowed to present as fact, and how to prevent them from spreading misinformation.

Biography

I hold the Chair of Ethics of Artificial Intelligence at the University of Graz (IDea_Lab), direct the Guilty Minds Lab, and am a research associate of the Digital Society Initiative at the University of Zurich. I studied Philosophy, Politics and Economics at Oxford and received my PhD from the École Normale Supérieure in Paris (Institut Jean Nicod), with fellowships at Princeton and NYU. I then held positions at the University of Pittsburgh, at Columbia University, and at the University of Zurich.

I have published close to fifty articles in journals including PNAS, Cognition, Noûs, Cognitive Science and Linguistics and Philosophy, and in the proceedings of CHI, CSCW and FAccT. Before philosophy I studied film at NYU’s Tisch School of the Arts and directed several films. I live in Graz and Zurich.
